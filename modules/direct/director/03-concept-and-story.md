# 03 · Concept and story

The concept is the whole job. A mediocre idea shot perfectly is still forgettable. A great idea survives rough execution.

## What a winning concept has

1. **One image people would screenshot.** A room full of bankers on a sofa. A lift that opens onto a front lawn. A museum case holding a 47-page form.
2. **A human truth.** Something every viewer has felt (the dread of the bank appointment, auction nerves, waiting on hold).
3. **A turn.** The product flips that truth in one beat.
4. **A reason for the wow.** The impossible moment *is* the product promise made visible (level 24 = 24 hours).
5. **A line** that could live on a billboard, 3–6 words.
6. **Local texture.** Specific places, habits and objects the audience owns (an NZ open home, a Saturday auction, pōhutukawa, a tūī).

## Anti-patterns (seen in failed drafts)

- **Metaphor stacks.** Room turns into a bank, then paper towers, then flying cranes. Each is "interesting", but together they need decoding and never show the product.
- **Feature parade.** Eight beats of equal weight, and nothing lands.
- **Symbolic product.** The product is a glow, a bird, a light, but never a real screen in a real hand.
- **Setup-heavy openings.** More than about 4s before we know who wants what.
- **Too dramatic for the category.** Home finance wants warmth and wit. Epic tragedy reads as a trailer.

## Idea generators (use two or three, then filter)

- **Flip the power.** Who normally holds it? Give it to the customer (banks bid on the buyer at an auction).
- **Literalise the promise.** "24 hours" becomes 24 floors, 24 frames, one day outside a window.
- **Museum or future.** Treat today's pain as a historical artefact (the Museum of Waiting).
- **One ritual, reframed.** Take a cultural ritual (open home, auction, moving day) and change one rule.
- **Time collapse.** The product compresses time: one oner across 24 hours, 1974 versus today match cuts.
- **Innocent witness.** A dog, child or grandparent notices the change first (the dog is already packing).
- **Scale shift.** A tiny miniature world, or one giant object in a real street.
- **Sing-along or national moment.** A song everyone knows, and the whole country joins (licensing cost).

## The "one engine" method (when every feature must be shown)

Choose a structure that naturally has stops, and assign one feature per stop.

| Engine | Stops | Example |
|---|---|---|
| A lift ride | Floors | Going Up: profile at floor 3, eligibility at 5, lenders step in at 9 and 11, approval at 24 |
| One day | Hours | A oner from 8am to 8am |
| An auction | Bids | Each bid is a feature |
| A museum tour | Exhibits | Each case is an old pain, and the last is the new way |
| A drive home | Landmarks | Each street is a step |

Rule: each stop is one line of dialogue or one screen, and 2.5–4s. The engine carries the pace, so features never feel listed.

## Logline format (pitch 3–6)

```
TITLE
Logline: 1–2 sentences, present tense, who + where + the turn.
The image: the one shot that sells it.
How the product lands: which promise and proofs, and where.
Why it wins: consumer reason + investor reason.
Line: 3–6 words.
```
End with a ranking and a recommendation. Ask the user to pick before writing the full shot list.

## Five anchors (lock before the shot list)

Emotion · visual motif · anchor object · the break · the final image. Example (Going Up): relief · the floor display · the unopened loan binder · "You're approved" at 24 · the lift doors opening onto the lawn.

## Checklist before presenting

- [ ] Can you tell the whole story in one sentence?
- [ ] Is the product a real screen or object at the turn?
- [ ] Is there exactly one wow (two at most), and does it express the promise?
- [ ] Does the audience know who wants what within 4s?
- [ ] Is it new to this conversation (not a re-skin of a rejected idea)?
- [ ] Could it be shot for real? (If yes, AI can make it look real.)
